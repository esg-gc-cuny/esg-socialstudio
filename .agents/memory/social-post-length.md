---
name: Social post length handling
description: Preserve readability while fitting short, platform-specific social posts.
---

Never fit a social teaser by cutting in the middle of a sentence. Prefer complete sentences; if an overlong hook still matters, shorten at a grammatical clause boundary. Reserve space for the source URL, hashtags, and call to action before choosing the hook length.

Start with a topic-specific teaser, not a generic announcement. Write in the publisher's third-person voice and integrate a verified author naturally when it strengthens the post, but do not force a fixed opening or byline. “Our” is allowed in natural publisher phrasing and the final CTA; avoid first-person singular, emojis, dash punctuation, and unsupported audience claims. Give enough context to create curiosity without summarizing the full article.

**Why:** The user repeatedly flagged posts as lacking context, accuracy, or reader appeal.

**How to apply:** Name the subject and include a concrete source-grounded detail, contrast, or open question. Explain the article-supported connection between them; never pair an unrelated fact with a generic question. Invite readers by naming what the article helps them understand, without inventing stakes or withholding context. Every sentence should add grounded context, attribution, or curiosity; cut filler.

Question hooks are optional: use one only when it makes sense for the source and platform, not as a default for every post.

**Why:** The user specifically asked for a question hook “only if it makes sense.”

**How to apply:** Consider genuine questions explored by the source; keep a natural declarative opening when a question would feel forced or introduce unsupported implications.

For attribution, use the verified author's name with an accurate verb instead of “the article states/says.” Preserve the source's level of certainty; if there is no verified author, state the supported point directly.

Never refer to “the article” in the teaser body. Use verified author names or discuss the subject directly; the fixed “Read the full article” link CTA remains the exception.

**Why:** The user repeated that the posts should sound like a professional publisher inviting followers, not a detached reviewer.

**How to apply:** Keep professional, curious platform-specific context, tease rather than disclose all findings, and avoid commentary about what the article does or does not settle.

**Why:** The user requested author-led attribution rather than treating the article as the speaker.

**How to apply:** Use verbs such as explains, examines, argues, or suggests only when accurate to the source. Do not force attribution into every sentence or invent a byline.

Keep the hook and concise context in one coherent, inviting teaser. The fixed full-article link is the final CTA, but the body must not read like statements of findings alone. Integrate a natural, specific invitation into the author/context sentence rather than adding a detached slogan.

**Why:** The user found link-only invitations too statement-like. Earlier rigid invitation templates also produced repetitive filler, so the invitation needs to be integrated and source-specific.

**How to apply:** Budget the final CTA and hashtags first. Connect the hook to what readers can discover through the verified authors' work, using a natural invitation rather than a neutral summary. Keep it platform-sized, source-grounded, and especially concise on Bluesky.

For ESG Social Studio, use one selected, platform-specific teaser per platform. Bluesky and Substack need enough standalone context to name the subject, ground it in a concrete article detail, and make the open question clear. A Bluesky opener must be a real source-supported hook, such as a meaningful tension, contrast, specific detail, or genuine open question, not just a topic restatement or compressed summary. If no true tension exists, lead with the sharpest supported observation rather than inventing one. On Bluesky, drop the byline and optional hashtags before cutting context to fit the link. Never invent facts or author names; keep the article's explanation and conclusions for the reader to discover.

**Why:** The user approved a simple one-draft-per-platform workflow and emphasized human, publisher-voice teasers that hook readers without giving away the full article. They also noted that Bluesky and Substack drafts need more context, requested an on/off option for hashtags, and said Bluesky drafts still lacked a real hook.

**How to apply:** Keep article analysis and alternate drafts out of the current UI unless requested. Preserve the full article-link line in the post and copy action. Hashtags are enabled by default but user-controllable; when enabled, add distinct article-specific tags up to platform limits, dropping tags before context or the final link. With the toggle off, return none. Prioritize a named subject plus concrete detail on Bluesky and Substack.

Write each platform independently for its audience, with its own tone, length, hook, invitation, and hashtag brief. Do not make a generic post and repair its voice afterward with a stock author/invitation prefix.

**Why:** The user explicitly requested four separate prompts and requests after repeated shared-prompt revisions and templated invitations still did not sound like inviting social posts.

**How to apply:** Preserve each platform's native editorial voice through validation. Questions remain optional. Budget Bluesky's full link first and combine context and invitation into a compact sentence when necessary.

Use a mix of recognizable subject hashtags and specific article-grounded tags where appropriate, rather than obscure compounds or generic promotional slogans.

**Why:** The user requested as many relevant, source-grounded hashtags as fit, rather than an arbitrary 1–2 tag target.

**How to apply:** Prefer standard topic names and useful location, industry, or policy tags explicitly supported by the source, up to each platform's maximum. Do not pad counts, add unrelated popular tags, or promise increased reach.
